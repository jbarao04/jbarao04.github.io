João Barão

Research Assistant at INESC-ID, Lisbon

Lisbon, Portugal

I’m a Research Assistant at INESC-ID in Lisbon, working on QSHIELD, a joint INESC-ID / CMU project funded by FCT, with Prof. José Fragoso Santos (INESC-ID) and Prof. Limin Jia (CMU).

I hold an MSc in Data Science from University College London, where I graduated with Distinction. My MSc thesis, supervised by Prof. Ingemar Cox, studied how to combine LLM and human relevance judgments to evaluate the best retrieval systems; a paper based on it has been submitted to ECIR 2027. Before that, I studied Artificial Intelligence and Data Science at the University of Porto, where I was the best student of the bachelor’s degree, with the highest GPA in the programme’s history.

My research interests lie in understanding what reinforcement learning (RL) teaches LLMs during post-training. In particular, I am interested in:

  • open-ended and long-horizon environments, where sparse rewards make training difficult;
  • the capabilities that RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) produces, and whether they transfer to other settings;
  • reward hacking, and measuring when a policy starts optimizing the judge instead of the task.

My main research question is how to extend RL post-training beyond verifiable tasks, to open-ended and long-horizon problems. One of my long-term goals is to develop systems capable of open-ended scientific discovery.

Outside research, I co-founded Miles AI, a startup backed by Sterling Road VC that builds LLM and retrieval systems for drafting replies to customer emails, and co-developed Vota AI, a chatbot on party programmes for the Portuguese general elections used by more than 50,000 people.

  1. UCL
    Efficiently Combining LLMs and Humans to Grade Test Collections for Retrieval Evaluation
    João Barão
    MSc thesis, University College London, Aug 2026
    Abs Bib PDF

    Test collections to evaluate and benchmark retrieval systems include different queries and text passages graded by how relevant each is to the query. These relevance judgments are grades a human assessor gives to each query-passage pair. They are, however, very expensive to produce and large language models are being increasingly used to grade query-passage pairs in place of human assessors. Test collections graded by LLMs produce rankings of retrieval systems that agree closely with the ones we would obtain from a human-graded test collection. However, the agreement falls when we restrict the comparison to the best systems being evaluated. This thesis asks how to efficiently combine humans and LLMs in grading a test collection so that the obtained ranking of the best systems is close to the one a fully human graded collection would give. An LLM can report when it is uncertain about a grade, and that could be used to decide which pairs to send first to a trained human assessor. The best signal we built predicts the per-query agreement with human assessors at a Kendall tau of 0.34. That is not enough to decide which queries to send back to a human assessor. To find out why, we analysed what a single grade error an LLM commits on a passage does to the ranking of systems. The damage on the ranking can be decomposed into two parts, one measuring how wrong the grade is and one measuring how differently the systems ranked that passage. This decomposition gives a selection rule to decide which pairs should be sent first to a human assessor. When sending only 9 to 14 percent, in four of the five years, of the pairs to a human assessor ordered by our proposed policy, the ranking of the twenty best systems agrees with the fully human ranking at a Kendall tau of 0.95.
